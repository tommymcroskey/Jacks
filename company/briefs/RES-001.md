# RES-001: explain where portfolio value comes from

Author: Quant Researcher agent. Project: P001. State: proposal awaiting independent review.

## Question and purpose

If an account becomes more valuable, how much came from investment profit and how much did its owner add? A deposit increases wealth inside the account without demonstrating investment skill. We need an accounting explanation before trusting any future strategy comparison.

**Cash** is currency held in the account. A **position** is the number of asset units held. A **mark** is the price used to value those units. **Equity**, or account value, is cash plus position times mark. **External flows** are deposits into or withdrawals from the account; purchases and sales are internal exchanges of cash and assets.

For this simple scope, profit over an interval is ending equity minus starting equity minus net external flows (deposits positive, withdrawals negative). Fees reduce profit. An unsold asset's price change can contribute profit even though that change has not yet become cash. Neither a deposit nor sale proceeds alone should be labeled profit. A percentage return needs an additional convention for flow timing; defer that measurement until the ledger is accepted.

## Proposed contract and hand example

Use one fictional currency, CU, and one long asset, with whole units and no borrowing. The following prices and events are invented teaching inputs, not market observations or measured results. Treat the opening balance as the interval's initial condition, not a new deposit within it. All entries below are exactly representable in hundredths of CU; engineering and risk must agree on precision for other inputs.

Process numbered events in order. A trade uses its explicit execution price; a valuation uses only the latest mark already available. Do not infer a new mark from a trade. Charge the fee with the trade. Assume immediate settlement, no interest, taxes, dividends, or splits. Defer corporate actions to a follow-up. These are simplifying proposals for review, not a production accounting specification.

| Event | Input | Cash CU | Units | Mark CU/unit | Equity CU | Cumulative net flow CU | Cumulative profit CU |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | Opening cash 100; mark 10 | 100 | 0 | 10 | 100 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | Buy 4 units at 10; fee 1 | 59 | 4 | 10 | 99 | 0 | -1 |
| 2 | Deposit 50 | 109 | 4 | 10 | 149 | 50 | -1 |
| 3 | New mark 12 | 109 | 4 | 12 | 157 | 50 | 7 |
| 4 | Sell 1 unit at 12; fee 1 | 120 | 3 | 12 | 156 | 50 | 6 |

Hand derivation: buying costs 4 x 10 + 1 = 41; the deposit adds 50 cash; the mark change adds 4 x (12 - 10) = 8 value; the sale adds 12 - 1 = 11 cash and removes one unit. Final equity is 120 + 3 x 12 = 156. Of its 56 increase, 50 is deposited money and 6 is profit: 8 price gain minus 2 fees. This illustration is a proposed expected result, not a tested implementation.

## What a future researcher needs

Retain ordered events, event identifiers, when each price became available, execution prices, quantities, fees, external flows, currency/units, and cash/position/equity after each event. Preserve source inputs and versions, assumptions, code commit, and reproduction commands. Real data would also require provenance and limitations; none is used here. Distinguish execution prices from valuation marks so an apparent gain can be traced to price movement, execution differences, fees, or mistaken data timing. Later strategy work needs an agreed baseline and realistic costs before evaluating performance. This brief establishes no trading edge.
